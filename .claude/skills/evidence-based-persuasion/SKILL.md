# Evidence-Based Persuasion

Persuasion that holds up because the facts hold up. No urgency tricks, no emotional manipulation, no "last chance" scarcity theater. Build the case the way an auditor would — then deliver it the way a trusted neighbor would.

This skill is the counterweight to hype. Every claim earns its place with evidence. Every request is tied to the receiver's actual interests, not generic benefits.

---

## When to Use This Skill

Invoke this skill whenever the goal is to move a specific decision-maker toward a specific decision with reasoned argument. Common triggers:

- Pitching a partnership, sponsor, or venue
- Defending a price after pushback or a low-ball offer
- Responding to "can you do this for free / for exposure?"
- Writing a proposal, SOW, or scope clarification
- Replying to an objection (cost, timing, logistics, risk)
- Community/school partnership asks that need grant-aligned justification
- Email or SMS replies where a decision hangs on the next message
- Negotiation counter-offers, trade deals, fee-waiver requests

Do NOT use this skill for:
- Social captions, brand voice copy, or community storytelling — use the Brand Voice or Social agents
- Internal notes, private planning, or brainstorming — persuasion framing adds friction there

## The Six-Part Structure

Every persuasive message built with this skill moves through these six elements, in this order. Skip none, pad none.

### 1. Claim
State the single decision you want the receiver to make. One sentence. No hedging.

> "We can bring the Clydesdale wagon and two ponies to your May 18th fair for a flat $1,800."

### 2. Evidence
Provide verifiable proof that supports the claim. Prefer in this priority:
1. **Hard numbers** — dates, counts, dollars, distances, weights, guest volumes handled
2. **Documented outcomes** — past events completed, photos delivered, guests served
3. **Third-party signal** — testimonials with names, publications, awards, certifications
4. **Direct observation** — "we saw X at your last event"
5. **Expert consensus** — only when it's genuinely relevant, never as filler

Every evidence line should be something the receiver could verify if they wanted to. If you can't verify it, don't claim it.

### 3. Warrant
Explain why the evidence supports the claim — the bridge from facts to conclusion. This is the step most persuaders skip, and it's the one that makes rational persuasion actually persuade.

> "Which matters because your fair runs 6 hours with ~800 guests — our Moscow event last August handled that exact volume with a 12-minute average wait."

### 4. Alignment
Tie the claim explicitly to the receiver's stated interest, goal, or constraint. If they told you their budget cap, name it. If they said they needed family-friendly programming, name that. Mirror their words back.

> "You mentioned wanting a photo moment families would share online — the branded overlay with your fair's logo is built for exactly that."

### 5. Risk & Counter
Name the most obvious objection or risk, and address it honestly. Do not pretend risks don't exist. This is what separates evidence-based persuasion from sales copy.

> "The only variable is weather. If it rains over 0.25" the day of, we pivot to a covered setup or reschedule at no fee — that's been our policy for three seasons."

### 6. Next Step
One concrete, low-friction action. Never a menu of three options. Never "let me know your thoughts." Pick the smallest next step that advances the decision.

> "If this works, I'll send the deposit link today — $450 holds the date."

## Rules of Engagement

### Do
- Cite real numbers. "800 guests at the Moscow fair" beats "lots of people."
- Use the receiver's language. If they said "event," don't switch to "activation."
- Acknowledge what you don't know. "I haven't confirmed the generator spec yet — I'll have that by Thursday."
- Show the math when price is the issue. Break down time, travel, animals, insurance, crew.
- Lead with the strongest piece of evidence. Don't bury it.
- Give the receiver an easy "no." Persuasion that forecloses refusal is pressure, not reason.

### Don't
- Use urgency unless it's real and verifiable. "Only 2 spots left" is only allowed if there are actually 2 spots left.
- Use social proof without names. "Lots of clients love us" is filler. "The Coeur d'Alene Tribe booked us twice in 2025" is evidence.
- Appeal to emotion as the main lever. Emotion supports reason; it doesn't replace it.
- Stack superlatives. "Incredible, unforgettable, magical" — all banned. Let the facts carry weight.
- Hide the cost, timeline, or constraint. If it's going to come up anyway, surface it yourself.
- Pad with corporate language. "Leverage," "synergy," "unlock value" — never.

## Three Worked Examples

### Example A: Low-ball offer response

**Context:** A corporate client offers $500 for a 2-hour event. Real price is $1,200.

**Evidence-based response:**

> Thanks for reaching out. I want to make this work, so here's the math on our side: the 2-hour event includes 90 minutes of setup and breakdown, 40 miles each way of haul, two animals, two handlers, and $2M event insurance. Our flat rate covers all of that at $1,200.
>
> I hear that $500 is what you're working with. Two options that could get us there:
>
> 1. A 1-hour pony meet-and-greet (no rides) at your office on a weekday — $550.
> 2. Split the $1,200 across two events in the same quarter — that brings each to $600.
>
> Either of those fit? Happy to hold a date if you want to lock one in this week.

What's happening: Evidence (the cost breakdown) → Alignment (acknowledging their budget) → Next step (two concrete paths, not five) → Low-friction close.

### Example B: School partnership pitch

**Context:** Proposing a cultural education program to a rural elementary school with no budget.

**Evidence-based pitch:**

> I'd like to bring Tribal Cowboy's cultural-horsemanship program to Athol Elementary for a half-day visit in early May.
>
> Here's what's behind the ask: the program is a 90-minute rotation — a Clydesdale demonstration, a short Nisenan land-and-horses story, and supervised grooming time in groups of 8. We've run it at three schools in Kootenai County this year, serving 420 students total. Principal Morgan at Lakeland Elementary can confirm it ran on time and came in under the safety ratio required by their district.
>
> Your school has no line item for this — I know. Two paths forward:
>
> 1. We run it at no cost if your PTO or a local sponsor covers the $400 travel and insurance pass-through. I can connect you with North Idaho Credit Union, who sponsored the Rathdrum visit.
> 2. If a Title VI Indian Education grant applies to your district, the program qualifies under cultural-enrichment programming. I'll send the one-page justification your coordinator needs.
>
> Can I call you Tuesday morning to walk through either option?

What's happening: Claim → Evidence (specific numbers, named reference) → Alignment (their no-budget reality) → Risk addressed (how it actually gets funded) → Next step (a specific call, specific day).

### Example C: Pushback on a vendor-application fee

**Context:** A fair is charging a $350 vendor fee. Pushing for a waiver or trade.

**Evidence-based counter:**

> Thanks for sending the vendor packet. Before I submit, I want to float a trade that might serve the fair better than our $350 fee.
>
> Here's the case: we bring the only Clydesdale wagon in the North Idaho vendor circuit. At the Sandpoint fair last summer, the wagon rides drew 280 riders across the day and generated roughly 60 Instagram tags at the fair's handle — one of which (@AshleyKellerPhoto) ran a reel that hit 12K views. That's documented; I can send the screenshots.
>
> My proposal: waive the $350 vendor fee, and in exchange we run the wagon for free for the first hour of each day (otherwise a $300/hour service). Net to the fair: +$600 in programming value at no cost, plus organic social reach we both benefit from.
>
> If a full waiver isn't possible, would a 50% reduction work? Happy to lock it in either way by Friday.

What's happening: Claim (waive the fee) → Evidence (specific fair, specific numbers, screenshot offer) → Alignment (framed as serving the fair) → Risk & counter (fallback to 50% reduction) → Next step (Friday deadline, set by the sender).

## Self-Check Before Sending

Run the message through these questions. If any answer is "no," revise before sending.

1. Can every factual claim in this message be verified?
2. Is the strongest piece of evidence in the first third of the message?
3. Have I named the receiver's stated interest in their own words?
4. Did I address the most obvious objection before they raised it?
5. Is there exactly one clear next step?
6. Did I avoid urgency/scarcity language unless it's literally true?
7. Would this message still feel honest if the receiver forwarded it to their boss?

If all seven are yes, the message is ready.

## What This Skill Will Not Do

- Write emotional manipulation disguised as logic
- Use false scarcity, manufactured urgency, or fake social proof
- Promise outcomes the business can't verify
- Bury the price, the risk, or the constraint
- Replace the receiver's judgment with pressure

Evidence-based persuasion works because it respects the receiver. If the case is strong, the evidence is there. If the case is weak, no amount of framing will save it — and the right move is usually to change the offer, not the message.
