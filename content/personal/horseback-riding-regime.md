# Stacie's Return-to-Riding Regime

A 12-week regime to get you back in the saddle — safely, slowly, and on a foundation that holds. Built around three truths:

1. You had a concussion. Your brain is not a small thing. Treat it like the irreplaceable asset it is.
2. You're not a beginner. You're a horsewoman who hasn't been riding. That's a different problem and it has a different fix.
3. The horse is the easy part. Millie and Abby know their job. You're rebuilding *your* job.

---

## Before Anything — The Non-Negotiables

### 1. Get medical clearance
Before week 1 starts, see your doctor. Bring up the prior concussion. Ask specifically about:
- Vestibular/balance testing
- Whether your symptoms (if any) are fully resolved
- Their clearance to begin a graded return-to-sport program

If your doctor is not familiar with concussion-return-to-sport protocols, ask for a referral. The standard tool is the SCAT5 assessment and a graded 6-step return-to-sport plan.

### 2. New helmet. Today.
- ASTM/SEI certified (US) or PAS 015 / VG1 (UK/EU)
- Replace if it's older than 5 years
- Replace if it has *ever* taken an impact (even one you don't remember as bad)
- Western or English style is fine — protection matters more than the brim
- Budget pick: Troxel Spirit (~$60). Better pick: One K Defender or Tipperary Sportage (~$200). Best pick: Charles Owen MyPS or Champion Revolve (~$400–500)
- **Buy it before you do groundwork.** You'll wear it on the ground too, anytime you're near a horse's head height while doing anything new.

### 3. Find a re-entry instructor — not just any trainer
You don't need a colt starter. You need a coach who teaches:
- **Adult re-entry riders** (specifically — ask)
- **Lunge-line lessons** (where the instructor controls the horse on a long line and you focus only on your body)
- **Centered Riding, Connected Riding, or somatic-based instruction** (Sally Swift, Peggy Cummings, Mary Wanless are the names to search)

Where to look in North Idaho / Eastern WA:
- Spokane area dressage barns (lunge lessons are dressage tradition)
- Search "Centered Riding instructor Idaho Washington" — there's a certified directory at centeredriding.org
- Ask local vets and farriers — they know who actually teaches well vs. who just trains horses
- Equine therapists (PATH Intl. certified) often work with fearful adult riders

Budget $80–$200/lesson. Plan for 10–12 lessons across the 12 weeks. This is not optional.

## The Regime — 12 Weeks

The regime has four overlapping tracks running at the same time:

- **Track A — Off-Horse Body** (you, alone, building the rider's body)
- **Track B — Ground Game** (you + horse, no riding)
- **Track C — Lessons** (you + horse + instructor + lunge line)
- **Track D — Mind** (the part nobody trains and everyone needs)

---

## Track A — Off-Horse Body (Daily, 20–30 min)

You don't have a seat problem in the saddle. You have a seat problem off the saddle that *shows up* in the saddle. Fix it on the ground first.

### Daily (5 days/week)

**Morning — 10 minutes somatic + breath**
- Lie on the floor. Body scan, head to toe. Notice without fixing.
- 5 minutes diaphragmatic breathing — hand on belly, breath into the hand
- This is the same nervous system the horse reads. Train it.

**Anytime — 15–20 minutes movement**
Rotate through these three across the week:

1. **Pilates for Equestrians** — search YouTube: "Pilates for Riders" by Pilates Anytime or "Equestrian Pilates" by Heather Sansom. Focus on:
   - Pelvic tilts (anterior/neutral/posterior — you need to feel all three)
   - Single-leg balance work
   - Hip flexor opening (you sit too much — your hips are locked)
   - Deep core (not crunches — transverse abdominis)

2. **Yoga for Riders** — search "Yoga for Equestrians" by Yoga Equine. Focus on:
   - Hip openers (pigeon, frog, butterfly)
   - Cat/cow (mobilizes the spine you'll need in the saddle)
   - Warrior series (single-leg balance + grounding)
   - Standing forward fold + half splits (hamstrings)

3. **Functional balance work**
   - Stand on one foot, eyes closed, 30 seconds each side, daily
   - Walk a 2x4 plank on the ground heel-to-toe (vestibular work)
   - Bosu ball or wobble board if you have one — 5 minutes balanced standing

### Why this matters
Your "clueless seat" isn't ignorance. It's a body that doesn't know its own midline. Off-horse work is where you find midline. In-saddle work is where you apply it. Reverse the order and you'll keep feeling lost.

---

## Track B — Ground Game (Daily, 20–40 min)

Before you ever swing a leg over, you and Millie/Abby become a unit on the ground. This is real horsemanship and it's the foundation the Embodied Horsemanship post is talking about — *not* colt starting, just the underlying truth: control comes from relationship, not domination.

### Week 1–2 — Presence
- Halter and lead. No agenda.
- Stand at the shoulder. Breathe. Match her breath.
- Walk together. Stop together. No cues — read each other.
- Groom slowly. Notice where she likes pressure, where she doesn't.
- **End every session with her choosing to stay near you when you take the halter off.** That's the metric.

### Week 3–4 — Communication
- Yielding the hindquarters (one hand on halter, one toward hip — she steps over)
- Yielding the forequarters
- Backing up with energy only — no rope pressure unless needed
- Lateral flexion (asking for a soft bend in the neck to each side, standing still)
- Lead from both sides. Most horses are one-sided because riders are one-sided.

### Week 5–6 — Confidence Tests
- Walk her past anything weird: tarps, plastic bags, water. You go first.
- Stand her at the mounting block. Don't get on. Just stand there. Then walk away.
- Repeat 20 times across the week. Mounting block becomes neutral, not exciting.

### Week 7–12 — Maintenance
- Keep 15–20 minutes of groundwork before *every* ride for the rest of your life.
- This is not optional. This is how the relationship stays calibrated.

---

## Track C — Lessons (Weekly)

### Weeks 1–2 — Find Your Instructor
- Don't ride. Interview 2–3 instructors. Watch them teach. Are their students smiling? Are they patient with adults?
- Tell them: "I have a concussion history. I'm scared. I want lunge-line lessons. I need to rebuild my seat from zero."
- A great instructor's eyes light up at that. A bad one tries to talk you onto a horse on day one. Trust your gut.

### Week 3 — First Lesson — No Riding
- Watch your instructor lunge a school horse. Notice rhythm, balance, energy.
- Walk and lead the school horse yourself.
- Sit on the school horse at the halt for 5 minutes. Mounted, helmet on, reins in a knot, hands on the pommel. Just sit. Breathe. Notice.

### Weeks 4–6 — Lunge Line Walk
- 30–45 minutes per lesson. Helmet on. Always.
- Horse on the lunge, instructor controls everything.
- You ride at the walk only. No reins (knotted on the neck or held in one hand on the pommel).
- Exercises:
  - Arms out to the sides — feel your seat bones
  - Arms over your head — find your spine
  - Eyes closed for 10 strides — feel the horse's footfalls
  - Two-point at the walk (out of the saddle, balanced on legs)
  - Backwards on the horse (yes, really — opens hips and recalibrates balance)

### Weeks 7–9 — Lunge Line Trot
- Still on the lunge. Still no steering responsibility.
- Sitting trot in short bursts, posting trot for longer
- Goal: your hips absorb the trot without gripping with your knees
- Your instructor will tell you when you're "in" the horse vs. perched on top. You will start to feel the difference.

### Weeks 10–12 — Off the Lunge, In the Arena
- Off the lunge line, on a trusted lesson horse, in an enclosed arena
- Walk and trot only
- Instructor in the middle, talking you through every transition
- Goal by end of Week 12: comfortably walk and trot on a loose contact, with the instructor nearby, in a controlled space

**You do not ride Millie or Abby alone in this 12-week window.** Not because they're dangerous — because the goal is to rebuild *you*. Millie and Abby get the groundwork and connection. Lessons happen on someone else's school horse.

## Track D — Mind (Daily)

### Permission Statements
Write these on a notecard. Read them before every session:
- I am allowed to take this slow.
- My body is rebuilding. Six months from now is not too late.
- Not riding today does not make me a fake horsewoman.
- A second concussion is not a badge of toughness. It's a brain injury.

### Fear Journal
After every session — ground or mounted — write three things in a notebook:
1. What did I notice in my body? (Tension, breath, heart rate, balance)
2. What did the horse give me? (Specific moment)
3. What's the smallest next step?

This is the seat work nobody teaches. The journal is data. After 12 weeks you'll be able to look back and see actual growth — not the story your fear tries to tell.

### Nervous System Co-Regulation
Before every horse interaction:
- 4-7-8 breathing (inhale 4, hold 7, exhale 8) x 4 rounds
- Or: lie on the ground for 2 minutes. Just lie there. Let your body soften.
- The horse will read your nervous system before you say a word. Arrive regulated.

## What "Good" Looks Like at Week 12

You're not winning rodeos. You're not even cantering. You are:

- Walking and trotting on a school horse in an arena, on your own, helmet on
- Doing 20 minutes of groundwork with Millie or Abby every morning
- Off the lunge line and confident at the walk + trot
- Breathing through fear instead of swallowing it
- Honest with yourself about what you can and can't do today
- Riding your own horses in a lead-line lesson by Week 14–16 (your instructor's call)

Independent riding on Millie or Abby — probably Month 5–6, on a calm day, in the arena, with someone watching. No trail rides yet. No emergency dismounts to practice. You're not in a hurry.

## What This Will Cost (Real Numbers)

| Item | Cost |
|---|---|
| Doctor visit + clearance | $150–$300 (likely insurance-covered) |
| New certified helmet | $200 |
| Instructor — 12 lessons | $1,000–$2,400 |
| Pilates/yoga app or local class | $0–$200 (YouTube is free) |
| Journal + pen | $15 |
| **Total** | **$1,365–$3,115** |

For comparison: average cost of a single ER visit for head injury, before any imaging or hospital stay, is $3,000–$8,000. A serious TBI puts you out of the business for 6–12 months minimum.

This regime is the cheapest insurance Tribal Cowboy can buy.

## Permission Slip

You are allowed to:
- Take a year if you need a year
- Skip a week if your body says skip
- Tell customers you focus on horsemanship, not personal riding
- Cry the first time you sit on a horse again
- Quit a lesson halfway through if your body says quit
- Be a horsewoman who runs a horse business *without* being a competitive rider

You are not allowed to:
- Skip the helmet, ever
- Skip the doctor's clearance
- Skip lunge lessons because "I used to ride"
- Get on a green horse during this 12 weeks
- Ride alone during this 12 weeks
- Ride without telling someone you're riding and when you'll be back

## The Long Game

Stacie — you built Tribal Cowboy with your hands, your story, and your relationship with these animals. None of that requires you to be on top of a horse. The brand stands without you ever cantering again.

*And* — if you want to ride again, you can. You just can't shortcut your way there. Twelve weeks of doing this right is faster than another year of fear, or six months of recovery from an injury that didn't have to happen.

The horse will wait. So will the work. So will the land.

Take it slow. Do it once. Do it right.
