# Relearning the Western Seat — A Study Guide for Stacie

Built around the work of **Pegasus Physio** (@pegasusphysio on TikTok), with western-specific
adjustments and a confidence plan for the lope.

Goal: a quiet, balanced, independent seat — and a lope that feels safe.

---

## 1. Who is Pegasus Physio and why use them

Pegasus Physio is run out of Tarporley (UK) by a Chartered Physiotherapist and ACPAT
Veterinary Physiotherapist who treats both riders and horses. They work on a
mechanical horse (Racewood dressage and eventing simulators) so they can isolate
what the rider's body is doing without a real horse compensating for it.

Their whole approach can be summed up in one sentence:

> **The rider's job is to find and hold a neutral, balanced pelvis in motion —
> in whatever saddle you're in.**

They are dressage/English-leaning in their examples, but the biomechanics are the
same for a western saddle. A western seat that "looks" right but has a tipped
pelvis or a collapsed side will still feel unsafe at the lope. That's why their
content is worth studying even though you ride western.

They also make **SymmFit** — clothing with deliberate color lines and panels
that highlight asymmetry. You don't need it to learn from them, but knowing
about it explains a lot of their videos.

## 2. The Pegasus Physio core lesson — the pelvis is the command center

Every cue you give a horse — leg, hand, weight — is generated from the seat.
If the seat isn't quiet and neutral, none of the other cues are.

**Three pelvis positions to know:**

| Position | What it looks like | What it feels like | When it's a problem |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Anterior tilt** | Tailbone up, lower back arched, belly forward | Hollow back, "showy" posture | Pushes the seat bones forward off the saddle, closes the hip angle, tips you onto your crotch (pubic bone) |
| **Neutral** | Tailbone down, weight on the two seat bones evenly | Stacked: ear / shoulder / hip / heel | This is the goal |
| **Posterior tilt** | Tailbone tucked under, lower back rounded | Slouchy, "chair seat" | Pushes the seat bones back, locks the hips, makes you feel behind the motion |

Pegasus Physio's specific note: **women tend to sit in anterior tilt** and need a
*slight posterior adjustment* to find neutral. If your lower back hurts after
riding or you feel like you're "perched on your crotch," that's the tell.

### How to find neutral (off the horse)

1. Sit on a hard chair, feet flat, no leaning back.
2. Rock the pelvis all the way forward (arched, anterior).
3. Rock the pelvis all the way back (slouched, posterior).
4. Find the middle — the spot where your weight rests evenly on the two seat bones,
   not the tailbone and not the pubic bone.
5. Phone on a tripod, record yourself from the side. Look at the line from
   ear → shoulder → hip → heel. That's neutral.

Do this 2 minutes a day for a week before you ride. The brain learns the spot.

## 3. The seat bones (what you actually sit on)

The two bony points at the bottom of your pelvis (ischial tuberosities) are
your seat bones. Pegasus Physio's rule:

- In **neutral**: weight rests on **both seat bones** evenly. This is the
  "three-point seat" — two seat bones + the front of the pelvis lightly
  contacting the saddle. In a western saddle, the seat is wide and dished, so
  the seat bones drop into the deepest part of the seat naturally if your
  pelvis is neutral.
- In **anterior tilt**: weight shifts forward onto the **pubic arch** (the
  bone between your legs). This is uncomfortable, unstable, and the
  number-one reason riders feel like they're going to come out of the saddle
  at the lope.
- In **posterior tilt**: weight shifts back onto the **tailbone**. Slams your
  spine. This is the "chair seat" western riders fall into when they're
  nervous.

**Quick check in the saddle, halt:**
Sit deep. Lift your knees one inch off the saddle (do not pinch with the
thigh). You should feel two distinct bony points making contact. If you only
feel one, you're collapsed to one side. If you feel a single broad pressure
in front, you're anterior. If you feel pressure behind the bones, you're
posterior.

## 4. How to sit the canter / lope (Pegasus Physio's specific teaching)

This is the most important section for you.

The old advice is "scoop with the seat" or "drive with the hips." Pegasus
Physio's view is that **scooping and driving create tension and lock the
hips**, which is the exact opposite of what a relaxed lope needs.

Their model:

> **The canter is a wave. Let the pelvis ride the wave passively.**

The stride goes:

1. **Push-off** — horse's hindleg drives, forehand lifts up. The energy
   travels through your seat. **Your pelvis tilts slightly posteriorly
   (back of pelvis drops a touch).**
2. **Suspension** — moment of float. Pelvis returns toward neutral.
3. **Landing** — diagonal pair lands. Pelvis settles back to neutral.
4. Repeat.

What your job actually is:

- **Allow** the motion — don't make it, don't block it.
- Hip flexors and hip extensors work **eccentrically** (lengthening under
  load) to control how far the pelvis tilts and how quickly it returns.
- Belly stays soft. Lower back stays free. Shoulders stay over hips.

The most common mistakes Pegasus calls out:

- **Stuck in one tilt** — pelvis frozen forward (anterior) or back (posterior)
  the whole stride. You bounce.
- **Too relaxed** — pelvis flops, no control, you lose balance.
- **Too still** — gripping with the thighs to hold the pelvis fixed. You
  bounce harder and the horse can't lift its back.

The fix is **mobility through control**, not stillness.

## 5. The western-specific adjustments

Pegasus Physio rides English. You ride western. Three things change:

| | English | Western (you) |
|---|---|---|
| Saddle | Flat, close contact | Deep seat, wide, dished |
| Stirrup length | Longer for dressage, shorter for jumping | Long — leg almost straight |
| Cue style | Contact, frequent pelvis aids | Loose rein, weight + leg, less micromanaging |
| Hip angle | More open in dressage, closed in jumping | Open. Almost vertical torso. |

**What this means in practice:**

- The deep western seat helps you. Once your pelvis is neutral, the saddle
  *holds* you in place. You don't have to fight to stay there.
- The long stirrup means your **heel under hip under shoulder under ear**
  alignment matters more than ever. If your leg is in front of your hip
  ("chair seat"), you are going to bounce at the lope. Guaranteed.
- A loose rein means you can't use the reins for balance. Your seat *is* the
  balance.

The fix for chair seat is the **stirrup-stand drill** (see Section 7).

## 6. The lope confidence playbook

You said you are nervous in the lope. That nervousness has a biomechanical
signature, and once you know the signature you can hunt it down in your own
body.

**What fear does to the rider's body:**

- Hip flexors contract → pelvis tips anterior → seat bones leave the saddle
- Shoulders roll forward → torso collapses → you tip forward
- Diaphragm tightens → breath becomes shallow → core can't engage
- Thighs grip → horse's ribs are clamped → horse can't breathe deep or
  round its back → horse gets quicker or jerkier → you get more scared

This is a feedback loop. The way out of it is **breath first, then seat**.

### The lope-prep checklist (run this every time before you ask for the lope)

1. **Breath**: 4 in, 4 out, three times. Drop the shoulders on each exhale.
2. **Seat bones**: feel two contact points. Drop weight into them.
3. **Heels under hips**: glance down. Is the stirrup directly under your hip,
   not in front?
4. **Soft thigh**: open the knee one inch. No pinch.
5. **Eyes up**: pick a point 20 feet ahead. Do not look down at the horn.
6. **Pick the lead**: outside leg back, slight weight to inside seat bone,
   kiss / cluck / cue.

### The "few strides" rule

This comes from western trainers (Horse & Rider, Downunder Horsemanship) and
it works:

> Lope **just a few strides**. Then come back to a trot or walk.
> Repeat. Repeat. Repeat.

Knowing the lope is going to *end soon* lets your body relax into it. The
relaxation is what teaches your seat the rhythm. Once you can do three
strides without holding your breath, push to five. Then ten. Then a half
circle. Then a whole circle. Build over weeks, not days.

**Do not** try to lope a whole arena lap on day one. That's how the fear
gets reinforced.

### The "loose rein lope" milestone

When you can lope a half circle with one hand on the horn (or pommel of the
western saddle) and the rein loose in the other hand, your seat has caught
up. That's the milestone to aim for. Don't skip steps to get there.

### Visualization

Trainer Lynn Palm and others recommend this: between sessions, sit on a
chair, close your eyes, and **mentally lope**. Feel the rhythm. Picture
yourself loping a quiet small circle with your shoulders back and breath
slow. The brain doesn't know the difference between a vivid rehearsal and
the real thing — you'll show up to the next ride already half-trained.

## 7. Off-horse exercises (do these on the ground)

Pegasus Physio's whole point is **most riding problems are fixed off the horse**.
The horse only reveals what your body already does.

### Daily, 10 minutes:

1. **Pelvic tilts on a chair** (2 min)
   Anterior → posterior → find neutral. Stop in neutral and hold 30 seconds.
   Breathe normally while holding.

2. **Seated seat-bone shift** (1 min)
   Sit on a hard chair. Lift one cheek slightly off. Feel the seat bone on
   the other side. Switch. This is the same weight-shift you use to cue a
   lead.

3. **Glute bridges** (2 min — 3 sets of 10)
   Lie on back, knees bent, feet flat. Lift hips. Squeeze glutes at the top.
   Don't arch the back — neutral pelvis even at the top. Builds the
   posterior chain that holds you in the saddle at the lope.

4. **Clamshells** (2 min — 2 sets of 12 each side)
   Lie on side, knees bent and stacked. Keep heels together, open the top
   knee. Builds glute medius — the muscle that stops you collapsing to one
   side in a turn.

5. **90/90 hip stretch** (3 min)
   Sit on the floor, one leg bent in front at 90°, the other bent to the
   side at 90°. Lean forward over the front leg. Opens the hip rotators —
   the single tightest area for adult riders.

### Twice a week, 20 minutes:

- **Pilates** or **yoga for riders** — focus on classes that name pelvis,
  spine, core. Pegasus Physio specifically recommends Pilates because of
  the precision around the pelvis.
- **Single-leg deadlift** (bodyweight, 2 sets of 8 each leg) — teaches
  one-sided stability, which is what asymmetry actually is.

## 8. On-horse drills (in your western saddle)

Do these at the walk and trot first. Don't take any of them to the lope
until you can do them at the trot without thinking.

### Drill 1 — Stirrup stand
Stand in the stirrups (balls of feet on stirrup, weight in heels), keep
upper body erect, just clear the saddle. Hold for 10 strides at the walk.
Sit back down softly into both seat bones. Repeat 5 times.

This fixes chair seat. It forces the leg back under the hip.

### Drill 2 — Two-point at the trot
A modified jumping seat. Lift off the saddle slightly at the posting trot,
weight in heels, hands light. 10 strides. Sit. Repeat. This builds the leg
position that makes the sitting lope possible.

### Drill 3 — One-handed walk and trot
Hold the horn (or saddle pommel) with one hand. Reins in the other. Walk
and trot full laps. Feel where you reach for balance. That spot is where
your seat is weakest.

### Drill 4 — Eyes closed at the walk
On a safe horse, on a lead line if needed. Close your eyes for 20 steps.
Feel the four-beat walk in your pelvis: left-right-left-right. This is the
single best exercise for "feeling" the seat bones.

### Drill 5 — Trot-walk-trot transitions
Don't go lope until your transitions trot → walk → trot are smooth and your
pelvis stays neutral through both. Lope is just a bigger version of this.

### Drill 6 — Lope to halt, four strides
Once you're ready: lope four strides, halt smoothly (sit deep, exhale,
relax the rein arm). Walk. Repeat. The halt is the safety valve. Knowing
you can stop in four strides removes 80% of the fear.

## 9. The 4-week relearning plan

This is a guide. Move slower if your body or the horse needs it.

### Week 1 — Off horse + walk only
- Daily 10-min off-horse routine (Section 7).
- Two rides at the walk only. Drills 1, 3, 4.
- Goal: feel two seat bones every time you mount up.

### Week 2 — Add the trot
- Off-horse routine daily.
- Two to three rides. Walk + trot. Drills 1, 2, 3, 5.
- Goal: post the trot with the leg under the hip and the pelvis neutral.

### Week 3 — Add small loping
- Off-horse routine daily.
- Three rides. Walk + trot + 3-4 strides of lope, then return to trot.
- Use lope-prep checklist (Section 6) every single time.
- Goal: lope four strides without holding the breath.

### Week 4 — Lengthen the lope
- Off-horse routine daily.
- Three rides. Lope half circle, then full circle.
- Add Drill 6.
- Goal: one full circle at the lope, breathing the whole time, on a loose-ish
  rein.

After week 4 — stop counting weeks. Just keep doing the off-horse work and
keep extending the lope a little at a time.

## 10. Red flags to catch yourself

Set up a phone on a tripod and record every ride from the side and from
behind. Look for:

- **From the side**: ear / shoulder / hip / heel — straight line? Or is your
  leg in front of you (chair seat) or are you tipped forward (anterior pelvis)?
- **From behind**: does the saddle stay centered? Or does it drift left
  (right-side dominant rider, very common) or right? That's the asymmetry
  Pegasus Physio teaches against.
- **Are you holding the rein for balance?** If your rein hand jerks every
  stride, your seat isn't independent yet.
- **Are you breathing?** Record audio. If you can't hear yourself breathing
  in the lope, you're not.
