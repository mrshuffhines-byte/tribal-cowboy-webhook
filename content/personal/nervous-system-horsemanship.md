# Nervous System Horsemanship — The Real Framework

A practical guide to becoming the kind of horsewoman who can regulate her horse instead of fight it. This is what the Embodied Horsemanship post was actually pointing at, underneath the colt-starting language.

This is also the most valuable skill you can build for Tribal Cowboy as a business. Horses that can stay regulated at chaotic events — drones, screaming kids, dogs, balloons, strangers reaching out — are your single biggest business asset. Train this in your herd and you have horses no one else has.

---

## The Core Truth

**You cannot regulate a horse from a dysregulated state.**

Your nervous system is the thermostat. The horse reads it before you ever touch the rope. If you arrive amped up, scared, distracted, in a rush — the horse mirrors you. If you arrive grounded, soft, breathing — the horse mirrors *that*.

This is not woo. This is biology. Horses evolved to survive by reading the herd's nervous system. You are now the herd. Whether you like it or not, you are leading from wherever your body is.

So the work has two parts:
1. **Regulate yourself.** Always first.
2. **Help the horse find regulation.** Through pressure-release, presence, and timing.

That's it. Everything below is mechanics.

---

## The Window of Tolerance — For Horses

Every horse has three zones:

**🟢 Green Zone — Thinking, Learning, Engaged**
- Soft eye, relaxed jaw
- Head at withers height or lower
- Tail relaxed, not clamped or swishing
- Lick + chew, blink, deep breath
- Curious — moves toward novel things
- *This is where learning happens.*

**🟡 Yellow Zone — Alert, Activated, Not Yet Reactive**
- Head up, eyes wider
- Muscles tightening
- Faster breathing
- Looking for the threat
- Still listening to you, but barely
- *This is where you teach them to come back down.*

**🔴 Red Zone — Reactive, Survival Mode**
- Whites of the eyes
- Spinning, bolting, freezing, blowing
- Cannot hear you — the thinking brain is offline
- Adrenaline is running the show
- *This is past the learning point. You missed the moment.*

**Your job:** keep the horse in the green zone. Catch them in the yellow zone before they hit red. Build the muscle of *coming back down* from yellow to green. That's nervous system rewiring. That's it.

## The Six Principles

### 1. Regulate Yourself First
Before you even pick up the halter:
- 4-7-8 breathing (inhale 4, hold 7, exhale 8) — 4 rounds
- Drop your shoulders, soften your jaw, feel your feet
- Ask yourself: "Am I rushed? Am I distracted? Am I scared?"
- If yes — sit down for 5 more minutes. The horse will know. Don't lie to them with your body.

### 2. Notice, Don't Fix
When you approach Millie or Abby:
- Watch her eye. Watch her breath. Watch her ears.
- Don't immediately ask for something. Just *be there*.
- 90 seconds of presence before you do anything. Sometimes longer.
- This is the foundation. The horse learns: "she sees me."

### 3. Pressure Has Three Levels
- **Suggestion** (energy, intention, the feel of you stepping toward them)
- **Light touch** (rope, hand, voice)
- **Active pressure** (actual physical asking)

You always start at suggestion. You only escalate as needed. You release the *instant* the horse tries — not when they finish, when they *try*. That timing is the entire skill.

### 4. Release Is the Teacher
The horse does not learn from the pressure. The horse learns from the *release*. If you release at the wrong moment — too late, too soft, not at all — you teach the wrong thing.

Practice this on the ground until it's automatic. Ask, *try*, release. Ask, *try*, release. The horse's brain is wiring "pressure → I tried → it went away." That's the entire game.

### 5. Approach and Retreat
For anything scary (tarps, plastic bags, mounting block, your fear):
- Approach the scary thing until the horse notices and starts to activate
- *Retreat before they hit red zone*
- Let them breathe, lick, chew
- Approach again — they go a little further this time
- Retreat again
- The window of tolerance expands every cycle

This is the opposite of "make them face it." Making them face it floods the system and teaches them to shut down or explode. Approach-and-retreat teaches them they can come down.

### 6. The Pause Is a Tool
When the horse gets worried — *stop asking for anything*. Don't add work. Don't add pressure. Just stop. Stand. Breathe. Wait for the horse to come back down.

Most riders make it worse here. They get nervous when the horse gets nervous and they keep cueing, keep moving, keep adding noise. The skill is the opposite: when the horse goes up, you go *down*. Quiet, still, breathing.

The horse needs to learn you're the calm one. You only get to be the calm one if you actually are.

## The Daily Practice — 20 Minutes With Millie

### Minute 0–3: You
- Stand outside the gate. Breathe. Drop in.
- Don't enter until your shoulders are down.

### Minute 3–6: Presence
- Walk up. Halter on. Don't lead off yet.
- Stand at her shoulder. Match her breath.
- Notice: is she with you, or is her mind somewhere else?
- Wait until her head lowers or she licks/chews. Then move.

### Minute 6–12: Yielding
Pick one each day, in rotation:
- **Hindquarter yield** — energy toward the hip, she steps over with the inside hind. Release the moment she shifts weight.
- **Forequarter yield** — energy toward the shoulder, she steps the front feet across. Same release rule.
- **Backing with intention only** — face her, raise your energy, no rope pressure unless needed. The instant she shifts back, you soften.
- **Lateral flexion** — light feel down the rope, ask for the head to come softly to one side. Release at the *try*, not the full bend.

### Minute 12–17: The Test
Walk her past one thing she's not sure about. Could be a tarp. Could be the mounting block. Could be a flag.
- Approach until she notices
- Retreat before she activates
- Repeat 3–5 times
- End on a moment where she's calmer than she started

### Minute 17–20: Close
- Stand with her. Don't make her do anything.
- Take the halter off.
- See if she stays near you. That's your metric.
- If she walks away — that's information, not failure. Tomorrow do less work, more presence.

## Reading the Horse — The Specific Tells

Train your eye. Print this and put it in the barn.

| Sign | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Soft eye, slow blink | Green zone, regulated |
| Lick + chew | Processing, coming down |
| Deep exhale or sigh | Releasing tension |
| Head lowering, ears soft | Calm, connected |
| Standing square, weight even | Balanced, present |
| Head up, hard eye | Yellow zone, activated |
| Ears locked forward, frozen | Alert, scanning |
| Tail clamped or swishing hard | Tension |
| Holding breath | High activation |
| Whites of eyes showing | Approaching red zone |
| Pawing, jigging, calling | Anxious, not regulated |
| Tight jaw, teeth visible | Defensive |

Your job: catch yellow signs early, give the pause, let them come back down before they escalate. Every time you do this, you wire in the response: *something happened → I came back down → my person was there.*

That's the rep. That's the whole thing.

## Connecting This to Riding

The reason you feel clueless in the saddle is the same reason a horse spooks: your nervous system is dysregulated and your body can't access its training.

The skill you're building on the ground — staying regulated, breathing through activation, finding center under pressure — is *literally the same skill* you need in the saddle.

When you mount this week (at the halt, helmet on, ground person present), here's the practice:

1. Sit down. Drop your shoulders. Exhale.
2. Notice your activation. Heart rate up? Shallow breath? Hands gripping?
3. Don't fight it. Notice it.
4. Breathe. 4-7-8.
5. Feel Millie underneath you. Match her breath.
6. Stay until you both drop.
7. Dismount before activation creeps back.

That IS the lesson. You're not "learning to ride." You're learning to regulate yourself while a thousand-pound animal is between your legs. Once that's automatic — *then* the seat and the reins and the legs make sense.

## Connecting This to Your Business

Every horse on your Tribal Cowboy roster needs this training. Here's why it's worth real time:

**Event horses that have nervous system regulation training are:**
- Less likely to spook at chaotic events
- Less likely to injure a guest
- More photogenic (soft eye, lowered head — *that's the photo*)
- Calmer at the mounting block for kids
- Easier on you when something unexpected happens

**The pitch for clients:** "Our horses are trained in nervous system regulation — they know how to stay calm in chaos. That's why they're safe for your event."

That's a real differentiator. Most pony party operators have horses that *tolerate* events. Yours can be horses that are *engaged and regulated* through events. That's a marketing story and a safety story at the same time.

Suggested: 20 min/day per horse on this work. In your herd, that's a lot of horses — but you don't have to do every one every day. Rotate. Even one session per horse per week beats no sessions.

## Who To Learn From — The Names

The lineage of this work runs deep. These are the people teaching it well:

### The Current Leader
**Warwick Schiller** — former competitive reining champion who pivoted entirely to nervous system based horsemanship. He has thousands of hours of free YouTube content and a paid platform (Warwick Schiller Performance Horsemanship). His *Journey On Podcast* is also free and excellent.
- YouTube: search "Warwick Schiller"
- Start with his "Connection Training" videos
- His phrase "the horse is always telling you the truth" is the entire mindset

### The Body-Based Coach
**Tristan Tucker (TRT Method)** — Dutch dressage trainer focused on teaching horses to "find their way back" from triggers. Heavy on body language and energy.
- Search "TRT Method"
- Especially good for spooky or reactive horses

### The Mystic-Pragmatist
**Linda Kohanov** — author of *The Tao of Equus* and *The Power of the Herd*. Bridges Indigenous and Western horsemanship traditions. Her work on emotional intelligence and herd dynamics will resonate strongly given your background.
- Book: *The Tao of Equus*
- Equine Experiential Learning organization: Eponaquest

### The Old-School Foundation
**Buck Brannaman** and **Ray Hunt** — the classic American horsemanship lineage. Less explicit about "nervous system" language but doing the same work. Buck's documentary *Buck* (2011) is required viewing.

**Mark Rashid** — energy and intention based, also in this lineage. His books *Considering the Horse* and *Horses Never Lie* are gentle, sharp, and full of stories.

### The Embodied Horsemanship Account You Screenshot
That's **Bri Lawyer** — she's part of a newer generation teaching this work to women on Instagram. Worth following. Her content is concentrated and shareable.

### The Indigenous Connection (Worth Naming)
The roots of nervous-system horsemanship trace through Indigenous horse cultures — the Comanche, Nez Perce, Lakota, and Plateau peoples — who built relationships with horses based on observation, presence, and respect long before any modern "natural horsemanship" branded itself. As Nisenan Maidu, you have a heritage lineage to draw on that doesn't have to be borrowed from anyone. **GaWaNi Pony Boy** (Cherokee) wrote *Horse, Follow Closely* — a foundational text on Indigenous relational horsemanship. Start there.
