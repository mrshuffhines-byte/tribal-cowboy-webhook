## Important — About the Program Category

The Pony Post Pre-K Sprint is an **Equine-Assisted Learning (EAL) program** — an educational literacy program built around a calm pony. It is **NOT** therapy, counseling, mental health treatment, or a substitute for any of those services. Activities support reading, vocabulary, syllable awareness, and classroom calm. They do not diagnose or treat any condition. Idaho regulates the practice of counseling, therapy, and psychology — The Pony Post stays firmly on the educational side of that line.

When describing the program: it's an **educational reading and animal-safety program** built around a calm pony. Kids learn to read body language, follow safety rules, and recognize when a horse is happy, grumpy, or scared. Never use the words *therapy*, *therapeutic*, *treatment*, *clinical*, or *regulation* in client-facing materials.

## What's Different About the Pre-K Sprint

Pre-K kids (3–5) don't need sight words yet. They need:

- **Vocabulary** — they're learning words as fast as their brains can build them
- **Print awareness** — books have a front and a back, words go left to right, pictures tell stories
- **Listening comprehension** — can they follow a story?
- **Pre-phonological awareness** — rhyming, syllables, beginning sounds
- **Letter recognition** — the very first letter of their name
- **Pre-writing** — drawing, mark-making
- **Real animal-safety knowledge** — how to be near a real horse, how to read her body language

This Sprint hits all seven. Without ever asking a 4-year-old to decode a CVC word.

**Idaho alignment:** Idaho Early Learning eGuidelines, Communication-Language-Literacy domain. The Pre-K curriculum doesn't use formal ELA standards — but every activity below maps to the state's pre-K literacy markers.

# Pony Safety & Body Language — The Core of the Program

Visit 1 teaches kids how to be safe around horses. This is the heart of The Pony Post — practical horse-handling knowledge dressed as a fun lesson. **It is not therapy or clinical instruction. It is animal-safety education,** the same way schools teach fire safety or bike safety.

## The 3 Big Rules

Every visit opens with these three rules. By Visit 4, kids recite them from memory.

1. **Inside voice.** No screaming. Ponies are prey animals — a sudden loud noise sounds like a predator to them.
2. **Walk, don't run.** Quick movement scares horses. Always slow.
3. **Tell the pony you're there.** Especially when you're behind her. Just say *"Hi, pony"* so she knows where you are.

## Pony Vision — The WHY Behind the Rules

A pony's eyes are on the **sides** of her head, not the front. This gives her almost 350° vision — she can see ALMOST all the way around her body. But she has two blind spots:

- **Directly in front of her nose** (between her eyes)
- **Directly behind her tail**

That's why we never walk silently behind a pony — she can't see you, you surprise her, and a startled pony might kick by reflex. This isn't her being mean. It's her being a horse.

## The 3 Ways a Pony Says "No"

Ponies have only a few ways to tell us they don't like something. All three can hurt a kid badly:

1. **Tail swat** — like swatting a fly. Stings.
2. **Bite** — a quick nip.
3. **Kick** — back leg, hardest to predict.

So we teach kids to READ the pony's body BEFORE she has to say no with her body.

## Pony Looks — The 6 Body Language Signals (Pre-K)

Each "look" gets a word AND a synonym. This hits Idaho ELA word-relationship standards (K.L.5) at the Pre-K level.

| What the pony shows | Word | Synonym | What it means |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Ears forward** | HAPPY ears | *Interested* | Curious, paying attention |
| **Ears pinned back/flat** | GRUMPY ears | *Mad* | Leave her space |
| **Soft, blinking eyes** | CALM eyes | *Relaxed* | She's at peace |
| **Wide eyes, white showing** | SCARED eyes | *Afraid* | Something's wrong |
| **Still tail** | QUIET tail | *Calm* | Everything's fine |
| **Tail swishing hard and fast** | MAD tail | *Annoyed* | Give her space |

## The 3 Warning Signs (always end the lesson here)

If kids see ANY of these, they stop and tell a grown-up:

1. **Ears flat against her head**
2. **Tail swishing hard and fast**
3. **Lifting one foot off the ground**

These are the pre-bite, pre-kick warnings. Reading them is the lifelong skill The Pony Post teaches.

# Pony Ears + Pony Hooves — The Two Callbacks

Two body-action callbacks the Pre-K kids learn at Visit 1 and use across all four weeks.

### 🐴 Energy UP — "Saddle up!" / Pony Hooves

When you want to get their attention or start a new activity:

- **Stacie:** *"Saddle up!"*
- **Kids:** *"Giddy up!"* + **stomp feet 3 times** + show **Pony Hooves** (curl fingers into fists, knuckles up, hold both hands close to the tummy — like the pony's front hooves when she paws)

Stomping ties to the syllable paw lesson. Pony Hooves keeps hands close to the body — no arms extended, no kids accidentally clobbering each other.

### 🤫 Energy DOWN — "Whoa, partners!" / Pony Ears

When the room gets too loud or too excited:

- **Stacie:** *"Whoa, partners!"*
- **Kids:** Freeze + cup both hands behind their ears (**Pony Ears**) — listening just like a horse listens

Stacie keeps them on Pony Ears for 5–10 seconds of quiet, then resumes the next activity.

Total flow: about 20 seconds. Works every time.

### Teaching both at Visit 1

Teach in the first 5 minutes of Visit 1, right after the visual schedule. About 30 seconds each:

> *"Two things to learn before we start. When I say 'SADDLE UP,' you say 'GIDDY UP' and stomp your feet three times and make your Pony Hooves like this. (Demonstrate.) That tells me you're ready to ride.*
>
> *When I say 'WHOA, PARTNERS,' you freeze and put on your Pony Ears like this. (Demonstrate.) That tells me you're ready to listen."*

- **Real-world animal safety:** How to approach a pony, what her body language means, when to step back

### Step 1 — Take the photos (1 weekend at the ranch)

Take **30+ high-res photos** of the pony in different positions and settings:
- The pony in the pasture, morning light
- The pony with her mailbag attached (use the Visit 1 costume)
- Mailbag open showing letters or papers inside
- The pony walking through trees (wooded trail behind the ranch)
- The pony on an open dirt trail
- The pony at a fence or gate
- The pony with hay
- The pony walking away (sunset / wide angle)
- The pony at the ranch sign

**No kid faces in the book.** Hands and arms only when needed. Keeps photo releases simple.

### Step 2 — Lay out the book in Canva (1 weekend)

- Canva Pro account: $13/month (cancel after layout is done)
- Template: "Children's Picture Book" — 5.5×8.5 size, 12 pages
- One photo per spread, one line of text per page
- Use a simple, friendly font: Caveat, Fredoka, or Quicksand (all free in Canva)
- Add the Pony Express stamp graphic to the title page
- Add Tribal Cowboy logo to the back cover
- Add space for the sponsor logo (variable per Sprint)

### Step 3 — Print (1 week turnaround)

**Option 1 — Local print shop** (Office Depot, FedEx, or independent):
- Saddle-stitched booklet, full color, 12 pages
- 5.5×8.5 size (letter folded in half)
- Quote at quantity 20, 50, 100
- **Expected cost:** $1.50–$3 per book at 20 quantity
- **Time:** 3–5 business days

**Option 2 — Amazon KDP** (print-on-demand):
- Free to set up, $0 upfront
- Per-book cost: $4–$8 for a 24-page softcover (note: KDP minimum is 24 pages — pad with sponsor pages + author bio + activity pages)
- Time: 4–6 weeks for initial setup, then 1 week reorder
- **Pro:** Real ISBN, real book on Amazon, professional appearance
- **Con:** Slower, more expensive per copy

**Recommendation:** Start with Option 1 (local print shop) for the first 2–3 Sprints. Once the format is proven, graduate to Option 2 for the polished version.

### Step 4 — Stock the mailbag

15 books per Sprint, plus 3 extras (kids who join late, demo copies, sponsor copy). Pony's mailbag holds all 15.

# Pony Welfare Notes (Pre-K Specific)

Pre-K classes are LOUDER than K-2 classes. Younger kids have less impulse control. The pony is going to be in a room where 15 kids might shriek with delight at once. Pick accordingly:

- Use the calmest pony in your herd, period
- Limit Pre-K visits to 3 per week (not 4–5) — the noise is harder on the pony than K-2 chaos
- ALWAYS have an adult chaperone between the kids and the pony — never let a Pre-K kid approach unsupervised
- Bring water for the pony and a hay net to chew on during the visit
- Required between visits: 24+ hours recovery time

**Costume welfare:**
- Test every costume element at home for 3+ sessions before the visit
- If the pony flinches at the flower garland, skip the garland — don't fight it
- Costume goes OVER the functional halter, never replaces it
- Costume comes off in the parking lot before loading her back into the trailer — she doesn't ride home in costume

# Final Note

The Pre-K Sprint works because it does five things at once:

1. **Kids build vocabulary** — 20 new words, all anchored to a real animal they touched
2. **Kids build pre-reading skills** — print awareness, rhyming, syllables, first letters
3. **Kids learn real animal safety** — reading a pony's body language is a skill they keep for life
4. **Kids own a book** — *The Pony Goes to School* lives on their shelf with the sponsor's logo on the back cover
5. **The pony has a job** — costumes give her a character every visit, and the kids see her as a working animal with real work to do

The signature moves at Pre-K specifically:
- **The book delivery at Visit 2** — the pony walks in with books for every kid. This is the moment teachers will call other teachers about.
- **The costume rotation** — kids count down the days because they want to see what the pony is wearing this week
- **The custom book** — every kid takes home a real book about the real pony they just met, with the sponsor's name on the back

Build those three things well and the Pre-K Sprint sells itself.
