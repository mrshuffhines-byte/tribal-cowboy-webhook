Tribal Cowboy Education Program. How to Design It. A Briefing for Stacie.

Format: Single-narrator podcast script. Approximately twelve to fifteen minutes when read aloud. Designed for ElevenLabs text-to-speech. No markdown formatting. Punctuation is set for natural speech pacing.

---

So here's the question we're working on.

You want to build something like Mobile Whinnies. A real education program. With ponies. In schools. Reading. Math. The whole thing.

And the question on the table right now is, how often do you show up? Is it a one-time visit? Is it weekly for a month? Is it monthly? Is it once a year? Because every operator in this space does it differently, and the answer actually matters. It changes how much money you can charge sponsors. It changes what schools say yes to. It changes whether the kids actually learn anything. So let's walk through it.

There are basically four options on the table. Let me run through each one honestly, the good and the bad, and then I'll tell you what I think is right.

Option one. The one-time visit. You show up once. Forty-five minutes. Read a book. Sight words. Pony. Done. The upside is it's the easiest yes you'll ever get from a school. A principal can green-light a one-time pony visit over coffee. Sponsors are easy too. Buy a visit for three hundred dollars. Done. The downside is brutal though. Kids forget you within a week. There's no real learning outcome you can point to. You can't tell a grant committee, "we improved sight word recognition," because forty-five minutes does not improve sight word recognition. It's a fun day. That's it. So one-time visits are great for library story hours. Great for Native American Heritage Month assemblies. Great for special events. But they are not the flagship.

Option two. The four-week sprint. You go to the same class. Once a week. For four consecutive weeks. Forty-five minutes each visit. Then you're done with that class for the year. This is what Equest does in Dallas. This is what Southern Reins does in Memphis. Both have been running for years. The upside here is huge. Repetition is what makes the learning real. Sight words stick when kids see them four weeks in a row, not once. The pony becomes a character the kids actually know by name. By visit four, they're hugging the pony's neck and telling their parents about Millie at the dinner table. The other big upside, it's a clean product you can sell to a sponsor. One sponsor plus one school plus one sprint equals one invoice. Simple. About two thousand dollars per sponsor. The downside, it's a heavier lift. Four trips to the same school instead of one. More wear on you. More wear on the pony. More logistics. But it's the model that works.

Option three. The monthly drop-in. You visit once a month for five to nine months. Builds a relationship. Sustainable cadence. The upside, sponsors can fund a whole school year of you. The downside is the kids lose continuity between visits. A month is too long. They forget. And calendar drift kills it, because every month some school holiday or field trip wipes out the visit and you end up doing five visits instead of nine. Monthly works for libraries and senior centers. It does not work for classroom learning.

Option four. The yearly visit. One big day per year. The upside, it's easy. The downside, it's not really a program. It's just a visit. It will not get you onto a school district's curriculum list. It will not win you grants. So we're skipping this one.

Okay. So that's the lay of the land. Here's what I think you should do.

Build your flagship as the four-week sprint. Run it once per year per school. Same class, four consecutive weeks, then you're done with that school until next year.

Here's why this beats everything else.

First, repetition. Real learning needs real repetition. Four weeks in a row is enough for sight words to actually stick. You can walk into a sponsor meeting or a grant committee and say, with a straight face, "this program measurably improves early literacy outcomes." You can back that up. You cannot back that up with a one-time visit.

Second, the sponsor product is clean. One sponsor. One school. One sprint. Around two thousand dollars. That's a single purchase order from a credit union or a local bank or a real estate office. Mobile Whinnies sells this exact unit. So does Equest. There is a reason for that. Sponsors do not want to commit to a school year. They want to fund a thing, see the thing happen, get their logo on the thing. Four weeks gives them that.

Third, and this is the magic of it, between the weekly visits you get to use a mechanic Equest invented and it is brilliant. You write letters from the pony to the class. Between visit one and visit two, the pony writes a letter to the kids. You print it out, fold it, and the teacher hands it out on Monday. The kids read it together. The letter says things like, hey kids, I miss you, can't wait to see you Wednesday, here's what I had for breakfast, by the way the word of the week is "and." Then between visit two and visit three, another letter. By the time you walk back in for visit three, you are not a stranger anymore. You are the pony's mom. The kids think the pony wrote them a personal letter. Between-visit touchpoints, basically free for you to create on your laptop, but they multiply the emotional connection by ten.

Fourth, it's sustainable. You can realistically run six to eight schools per year as one person. Two sprints in the fall, two in the winter, two in the spring. Six schools, six sponsors, two thousand dollars each, that's twelve thousand dollars of sponsor revenue from the education program alone. Before grants. Before any of the downstream paid bookings that come from the exposure. Just from sprints.

Fifth, a four-week commitment is short enough that principals say yes. A yearlong commitment scares them. Four weeks feels manageable.

Okay. So the flagship is the Sprint. But you don't just offer one product. You offer a menu, and let schools pick. Here's the menu.

Product one. The Reading Sprint. Your flagship. Four weekly visits, forty-five minutes each, K through second grade. Sight words. Read-aloud. The pony delivers vocabulary words during a relay activity. Between visits, the pony writes letters to the class. In November, the cultural component talks about how horses came to the Plateau tribes after Spanish contact. Two thousand dollars to the sponsor. Completely free to the school.

Product two. The Math Sprint. Same format, four weekly visits, but the focus is financial literacy for K through third grade. Steal Mobile Whinnies' best idea here. They have a financial literacy mini named Knox. You do the same thing, but you make it about a working ranch. How much does it cost to feed a Clydesdale for a year? Spoiler, it's a shocking number, and kids love that. Budget. Expense. Income. Savings. Real numbers, real horse, real lesson. Two thousand dollars to the sponsor.

Product three. The Heritage Visit. This one is just a single visit. Sixty minutes. Timed to Native American Heritage Month in November, or to Idaho Day in March. One pony, one cultural lesson, one read-aloud, one Q and A. Five hundred dollars to the sponsor. This is your trial offering. Schools that loved the Heritage Visit become your Reading Sprint customers next year.

Bonus product four. Library Story Hour. Monthly drop-in at North Idaho libraries. Forty-five minutes. Open to the public. Easy to maintain. A whole year of monthly library visits gets sponsored by the library system itself or by one sponsor for the year, around fifteen hundred dollars. This is your community visibility play. It's where parents in North Idaho first see you. And it feeds the funnel for your paid pony parties and family photography.

So that's the menu. Sprint, Sprint, Heritage Visit, Library.

Now. The photo thing. You brought this up and you're right, schools usually will not let you photograph kids' faces. That's normal. So here's how the photo amplification still works even when you can't shoot the kids directly.

One. You shoot wide. The pony in the center of the room, kids visible from behind, or out of focus. This is fine for your Instagram and your sponsor reports. No faces required.

Two. You shoot the teacher with the pony. Teachers love this. Get their permission, snap one nice photo of them with the pony, frame it, send it as a thank-you gift. This is also your case-study material.

Three. You shoot the setup. The pony standing next to your sight word cards. The pony with the read-aloud book. Empty classroom, no kids, but the visual story of the program is still there.

Four. The school distributes the kid photos, not you. You give the teacher a class photo on a thumb drive, they hand it out through the school newsletter. You stay out of the parent-distribution loop entirely.

Five. Every kid leaves with a one-pager that goes home in their backpack. It's a thank you card from the pony. It says, thanks for hosting me today, I had fun, my favorite word was "and," my owner does pony parties and family events at the ranch in Athol if your family ever wants to visit, our website is tribalcowboy dot com. Fully PG. No kid photos required. Parents see it the same night when they unpack the backpack.

Six. And this is where the photography business actually wins. At the end of the school year, or as the capstone of the Sprint, the class comes to you. Field trip to your farm in Athol. On your property, with your photo releases in hand, you can shoot whatever you want. The pony, the kids, the Clydesdale, the wagon. That field trip is where the photography deliverable lives. That's where the funnel pays you back.

So your funnel just moves. It moves from, parent gets a branded photo the same day, to, kid takes a one-pager home, parents see it, parents come to the farm for a paid family session in the summer. The end result is the same. You just shift the touchpoint.

Okay. Here is what I want you to do this month.

Run two pilots. Pick one homeschool co-op, run a four-week Reading Sprint there. That gives you real evidence. Real testimonials. Real photos of the program in motion, even if they don't show kids' faces. Then pick one private school or library, run a single Heritage Visit. That tests the one-time format as a trial offering. Two products, two pilots, four weeks of work.

The homeschool co-op is the easiest yes you'll ever get. Cabrini Co-op in Coeur d'Alene. Arrows Co-op. Inland Northwest Christian Homeschoolers. Any of them. One parent's approval, no district paperwork. Email them the outreach email we drafted. Run the Sprint. Document everything.

By the end of June, you'll have a case study with two teacher quotes, two parent quotes, photos of the pony in two different classrooms, and a one-page sponsor pitch sheet. With that in hand, you walk into Mountain West Bank, Idaho Central Credit Union, Litehouse, Kootenai Health, and the Coeur d'Alene Casino. You pitch them on sponsoring a school's Sprint for the fall. Two thousand dollars per school. They write the check, you do the program, the kids learn to read, the sponsor gets community goodwill, and you have a real business inside a real business.

Last thing. The flagship name. The placeholder is Saddle Up and Read, but think about whether you want to name it after one of your horses instead. Gentle Carousel in Florida named their program after a single horse, Officer Magic, and that horse is now a sworn officer of the Ocala Police Department and has her own children's book. Mobile Whinnies has Coconut. Equest has Mini Ambassadors. If your flagship animal is Millie the Clydesdale, the program could be called Reading with Millie. Big horse, little reader. People remember an animal's name forever. They never remember a program name.

That's the design. Sprint as the flagship. Heritage Visit as the trial. Library story hour as the community play. Pen pal letters between the Sprint visits to multiply the connection for free. Two pilots in the next thirty days at a homeschool co-op and a private school. Field trip to your farm as the photo deliverable. Sponsors fund it. Grants fund it. Downstream paid bookings reward you for showing up.

That's the plan. Let's go build it.

End of episode.
