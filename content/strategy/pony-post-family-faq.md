# The Pony Post — Family FAQ
## For schools to share with parents BEFORE the first visit
### One-page handout, ready to print or email home

**Status:** ✅ Brand Voice approved (8.5/10) — ship as-is in backpacks or school email
**Why this exists:** The Council flagged that a small number of parents at any school will raise concerns about animals in enclosed spaces with young children. Allergies. Fear. Hygiene. Safety. A brief, confident, informative document distributed BEFORE the first visit eliminates 90% of those concerns — and gives the teacher cover when one comes up.

**How to use:** The school sends this home in backpacks (or by email) the week before the first visit. Optional: include a brief teacher note ("We're excited to host this program — here's everything you need to know").

## ⬇️ THE HANDOUT BEGINS BELOW ⬇️

# 🐴 The Pony Post is coming to your child's classroom

Hi there,

Your child's class is starting **The Pony Post** — a four-week reading program we run from our ranch in Athol, Idaho. A real, small, very calm pony will visit the classroom once a week for four weeks. She's about the size of a Labrador and considerably more polite.

Between visits, every child will receive a letter from the pony with a clue about what's coming next.

Here are the questions parents ask us most. We figured we'd answer them before you had to dig for them.

---

## How big is the pony?

Small. Around 200 pounds, knee-high to most adults. She's trained for classroom visits and has been to schools before. She's not a horse you ride.

## How calm is she, really?

Very calm. She's been working with children for years. She stands quietly while kids gather around her, accepts gentle petting on her mane and shoulder, and is unbothered by classroom noise. We don't bring ponies into classrooms unless they've been consistently calm in that kind of setting.

## Is my child going to touch her?

Petting is always optional, and it only happens with an adult standing right beside them. The visit teaches kids to approach a horse safely — from the side, slowly. Children pet her mane and shoulder under direct supervision. Watching from a distance is just as valid as petting. Some kids take a few visits before they want to reach out, and that's exactly how it should go.

## My child is afraid of horses. Can they still participate?

Yes. Plenty of kids come in nervous and spend the first visit watching from across the room. That's completely fine — the program is built so no one ever feels pushed. Your child can watch from a chair at the back of the room, hold the pony's letter without ever standing near the pony, or color in their activity book while the rest of the class meets her. Every part of the program — the stories, the letters, the activity book — works whether they're petting the pony or watching from a chair.

Please let the teacher know in advance so they can have a quiet spot ready.

## My child has allergies.

Tell the teacher in advance. Some families with mild horse allergies are fine because the visit is short and the pony is small. Others prefer to stay back. We don't bring shavings or hay into the classroom — only the pony herself, on a clean mat. If your child has a severe allergy to horses or hay, we'll work with the teacher to find a safe arrangement: observing from the hallway, watching a recorded version of the visit, or skipping the in-class portion entirely while still receiving all take-home materials.

## What about germs and hygiene?

We groom the pony before every school visit. Her hooves are cleaned. She's healthy and on a regular veterinary schedule. After the visit, we wipe down any surfaces that need it. Children wash their hands after the visit, the way they would after touching any animal.

## What happens if the pony has an accident in the classroom?

She doesn't — but we're prepared if she did. We bring a full cleanup kit on every visit, and she's taken outside to relieve herself before entering the classroom. In four-plus years of school visits, this has never happened indoors.

## What will my child be learning?

The program is built on the Science of Reading. Over four weeks, your child will:

- Write their own name on a real envelope
- Learn to count the beats (syllables) in words by clapping along with the pony
- Hear four read-aloud stories
- Learn how to safely be near a horse and read her body language
- Build their own activity book that they decorate, stamp, and personalize across all four weeks
- Learn about pit ponies — real small horses who worked in Idaho's Silver Valley silver mines — as part of a local history week

Every activity is aligned to the Idaho Content Standards for Kindergarten ELA and the Idaho Early Learning eGuidelines.

## What does my child bring home?

- Their **Pony Pals Adventure Book** — decorated by them across the four weeks
- **Four Pony Post letters** in stamped envelopes (these are program letters, hand-delivered by the teacher — not real mail)
- Themed take-homes from each visit (a paper miner's helmet, a paper detective badge, a small magnifying glass, a foil "silver" rock)
- A **photo with the pony**, if you give photo permission

## Does this cost anything?

No. The program is free for your family and free for your school. A local business has sponsored the four-week program at your child's school, which covers everything.

## Photo permission

The school will send home a Photo Release form with three options:

- ☐ Yes, my child may be photographed and photos may be shared in marketing or social media
- ☐ Yes, my child may be photographed, but photos may only be used by the school
- ☐ No, please do not photograph my child

Please return the form. If we don't have a signed form for your child, we default to no photography. You can change your answer at any time during the program.

## Is this a religious or political program?

No to both. This is a reading program about a pony. There's no ideology here — just literacy, local history, and a very patient horse.

## Is it therapy?

No. This is a reading program, not a counseling program. The pony is there to make learning feel less like school — not to provide any kind of treatment.

## Who runs it?

The Pony Post is run by **Stacie Hines** of **Tribal Cowboy LLC** in Athol, Idaho. Stacie is Nisenan Maidu, and she's been bringing horses into schools and communities across North Idaho for years — this program grew out of that work.

## INTERNAL NOTES — DO NOT INCLUDE IN HANDOUT

**Format for printing:**
- 8.5×11, single-sided OR double-sided depending on school preference
- Plain paper, clean layout — this should not look like a flyer, it should look like a school communication
- Tribal Cowboy logo small in the header or footer — not dominant
- Sponsor logo at the bottom, with the line: *"This program was made free for your child's school thanks to [SPONSOR NAME]."*

**Distribution timing:**
- Send home **7 days before** the first visit
- Schools may also email it through their normal parent communication channel
- Include a brief teacher note at the top if the school wants ("Mrs. Smith's class is excited to start The Pony Post — here is what your family should know.")

**What this document is NOT:**
- It is NOT a photo release. That is a separate form the school provides.
- It is NOT a liability waiver. Schools do not require waivers for in-class animal programs since the school's general liability covers it, but the school's existing field-trip-style waiver workflow may apply.
- It is NOT a sales document for sponsorship or future Tribal Cowboy events. Keep it strictly informational.

**Common parent questions NOT included (and why):**
- Cost (already answered)
- "Can my child ride the pony?" — answered indirectly ("She is not a horse you ride") and intentionally not given a separate Q because asking creates the expectation
- Religious/cultural concerns — addressed briefly and warmly; we don't go deeper because that invites more debate than it resolves

**Run this through the Brand Voice agent before first distribution.** The tone matters more in this document than in any other. It is the first impression Tribal Cowboy makes on parents who have not chosen to engage — they're reading it because their kid's teacher sent it home.
